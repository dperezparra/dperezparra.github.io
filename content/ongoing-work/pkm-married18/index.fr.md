abstract: >
  Growing evidence documents the gendered costs of war, yet we know
  comparatively little about which post-conflict policies foster women's
  long-run development. This paper estimates the causal effect of UN
  peacekeeping on women's human capital in post-war Sierra Leone. Linking
  geocoded data on UNAMSIL deployment sites to nationally representative
  demographic surveys, we exploit spatial and cohort variation in exposure to
  identify the impact of peacekeepers on women's education. We find that
  exposure to peacekeeping operations (PKOs) increases women's education by
  about 0.8 years, with gains concentrated at the secondary level — including
  higher secondary attendance and completion. We also document reductions in
  child marriage and teenage pregnancy. By contrast, we find limited evidence
  that these improvements operate through broad changes in conflict intensity,
  local economic recovery as proxied by nightlights, school construction, or
  development aid. Instead, heterogeneity patterns are most consistent with
  rehabilitation activities and gender-specific security mechanisms that
  lowered barriers to girls' schooling.
